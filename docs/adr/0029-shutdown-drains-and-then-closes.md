# Shutdown stops accepting, drains what is running, and closes the rest when its context ends

`app.Shutdown(ctx)` stops accepting connections, closes idle keep-alive connections at once, lets requests in flight finish while marking their connections to close after the response (`Connection: close`), and sends every WebSocket a close frame with status 1001 (going away), each after a short random delay so the clients do not all reconnect in the same millisecond. Whatever is still open when `ctx` is done is closed. The framework installs no signal handler: catching SIGTERM and calling `Shutdown` is two lines of the application's own Go, and a library that takes over signals surprises the program that embeds it.

**A delay before the listener closes** (decided and built on 2026-11-01, [ADR 0052](0052-health-and-readiness-follow-shutdown.md)). With `WithShutdownDelay(d)`, 0 by default, Shutdown first fails readiness and keeps serving for `d` with the listener open, so a balancer that learns of the deploy late stops sending before connections are refused; then it does everything above. The delay is bounded by half of what is left of Shutdown's context, as the WebSocket spread is, so the drain still has time, and a second Shutdown during it does not restart it. As built it lives in `App.Shutdown` on the app's clock, before the engine's Shutdown, which it does not change; `Close` ends it (`TestReadyFailsFromTheMomentShutdownStartsAndTheDelayKeepsServingUntilItEnds`, `TestTheShutdownDelayIsBoundedByHalfOfWhatIsLeftOfShutdownsContext`, `TestASecondShutdownDuringTheDelayWaitsForTheSameDelay`, `TestCloseDuringTheShutdownDelayEndsIt`).

**Streams at Shutdown.** An SSE stream never ends by itself, so waiting for it would spend the whole Shutdown context on every deploy. When Shutdown starts, the context of every live stream (`SSE`, and a `Stream` declared `Live`) is cancelled with the cause `ErrServerClosed`; a plain `Stream` drains like any request in flight ([ADR 0030](0030-streaming-is-a-return-type.md)). The starter of a stream sets its state and then reads the closing flag, and Shutdown sets the flag and then walks the connections, so a stream that begins during Shutdown is seen by one side or the other. It costs one atomic store when a stream starts and nothing on the plain path. Evidence: [`docs/research/decisions-websocket/`](../research/decisions-websocket/report.md), section R5.

**How the 1001 is sent, as built (step 13).** Shutdown walks the connections once under the server's lock and sorts them by state: idle ones are closed (a parked one is closed and then woken, so its own goroutine ends it and gives back its place; [ADR 0005](0005-our-own-http1-engine-with-a-goroutine-per-connection.md)), a connection in `stLive` has its request's context cancelled with `ErrServerClosed`, and a connection in `stWS` is collected. Outside the lock each collected socket is told `goAway(delay)`, which sends `Close 1001 "server is shutting down"` through `beginClose` (once; later data frames are dropped; the connection ends at the client's answer or after `CloseTimeout`, 5 s, on the wheel), so `OnClose` sees status 1001 with `Remote` false, whether the client answers or not. The jitter is uniform in `[0, window)`, where the window is 1 ms a socket (`GoAwayPer`), at most the spread (`GoAwaySpread`, 3 s unless the application gave `WithGoAwaySpread`), and at most half of what is left of Shutdown's context so the closes finish inside it; with the default 20,000 sockets are told to leave over 3 s, about 6,700 a second (arithmetic, not measured against a replacement instance), and 100 sockets are not delayed at all. A delay shorter than the wheel's one-second tick is sent at once instead of being rounded up to a tick, so a few sockets, or a test, are not made to wait. The wait reuses the socket's heartbeat node on the wheel (no new field of any size, no `time.Timer`; the one flag `leaving` fits the padding after the existing flags and `sizeof(WS)` stays 512), and the heartbeat does not re-arm it once the socket is leaving. Nothing is sent to a socket the application already closed. Calling Shutdown twice tells nobody twice. A parked WebSocket needs nothing more: the 1001 is written by the flusher, never by the reading goroutine, and the client's answer, or the close deadline that closes and wakes it, gives the socket a goroutine to run `OnClose` on (`TestShutdownSendsAParkedWebSocketItsClose1001AndOnCloseSeesIt`, parked and not).

**How wide the spread is, and who chooses it** (decided 2026-11-04). `WithGoAwaySpread(d)` sets the ceiling of the window, the one number an application can know and the framework cannot: how many reconnects a second the instances that remain can take. The rule stays 1 ms a socket, so the window is `min(sockets x 1 ms, d, half of what is left of Shutdown's context)`; the default `d` is 3 s, so an app that does not give the option behaves as before. `d` is a ceiling and not a delay: 2,000 sockets under a `d` of 10 s are spread over 2 s, not 10 s, so a deploy of a small app is not slowed by a setting made for a large one. It must be positive and given once (Registration refuses both, naming where). The option is checked for what Registration can know. Whether `d` fits inside Shutdown's context cannot be, because the context exists only at Shutdown, so it is stated instead: the window is computed when the engine's Shutdown begins, after the `BeforeShutdown` of the Services and after `WithShutdownDelay` have spent their share of the context ([ADR 0079](0079-services-start-after-the-app-binds-and-stop-in-reverse-around-shutdown.md), [ADR 0052](0052-health-and-readiness-follow-shutdown.md)), and half of what remains still bounds it. Nothing is shortened in silence: when it has WebSockets to tell, Shutdown logs one line, `bites: shutdown: WebSocket Close 1001 spread`, with `sockets`, `window` (the one used) and `asked` (what the rule gave before the context's half); when the half cut the window shorter than `asked` the line is a warning, says `cut short by half of what is left of Shutdown's context`, and names the two ways out (a longer context, or a shorter `d`). The start-up log lists `websocket shutdown close spread, at most`, marked default or custom, when the app has a WebSocket route. Cost: none on a request or an idle connection; `WSConfig.GoAwaySpread` already existed, and the option only fills it. The axes of [ADR 0003](0003-the-trade-budget.md) are not moved.

Scenarios, as arithmetic (a plan, not a measurement against a replacement instance). *20,000 sockets, `d` of 10 s, a 30 s context:* the rule gives 20 s, `d` caps it at 10 s, half of 30 s is 15 s, so the window is 10 s and about 2,000 clients a second reach the other instances, against 6,700 a second with the default. *The same with a 4 s context:* half is 2 s, so the window is 2 s and about 10,000 a second, which is worse than the default's 3 s would have been if the context allowed it; the option cannot buy time the context does not have, and the warning line is how the operator learns it: the answer is a longer grace period in the orchestrator, not a larger `d`. *A client that reconnects at once:* the window spaces the Close frames, so the arrival rate at the other instances is the rate of the closes; a client that retries with no back-off after a 1001 comes back as fast as it was told to go, and a client that also retries a failed connect in a loop can undo the spread. The window is a promise about when the server speaks, not about when the clients arrive.

What this fails at. The spread is uniform: it does not know the capacity of the other instances, does not look at their load and does not ramp, so a `d` is a guess made once by the operator. It is one number for the app, not per route. The window is not repeated for a socket that opens after it was fixed (a handshake in flight draws its delay from the same window, so those sockets are told within it). A second Shutdown logs the line again and tells nobody twice. A `d` above half of the grace period is honoured only up to that half, and the log is the only place that says so.

**What was rejected.** *Deriving the window from `WithShutdownDelay`:* the delay is how long the listener stays open for a balancer that learns late, a number about the balancer; the spread is a number about the capacity of the next instances. They are tuned apart (a 5 s delay with 20,000 sockets wants a 10 s spread, a 0 s delay behind a fast balancer with the same sockets wants the same 10 s), and tying them would make one setting change two behaviours and an application without a delay unable to widen the spread. *Two options, the window per socket and its cap:* a second spelling of one knob; the per-socket 1 ms is what makes a small app need no setting, and an application that wants a gentler rate per second sets the ceiling, which is the same lever for the sockets it has. *Exposing the rate (closes a second) instead of a window:* it reads well but hides that the context bounds the result, and the window is what the code, the log and the half-of-context rule already speak. *Refusing a `d` that cannot fit Shutdown's context:* the context is known only at Shutdown, so it is the log that says so. *A configurable fraction of the context in place of one half:* a second knob for a bound that exists so the closes can finish and be answered; nobody needs it until a measurement says so.

**A handshake in flight.** A request that is upgrading when Shutdown starts is not idle, so the walk does not see it as a WebSocket. The upgrade path sets the connection's state to `stWS` and then reads the closing flag, as Shutdown sets the flag and then walks, so one side sees the other. The 101 is written and the socket opens as any other (the route's handler has already run and its `OnClose` will run), then it is told to go with a delay drawn from the same window. A refused handshake is a plain response and drains like any request.

**A live SSE stream cut by Shutdown ends cleanly; any other live stream does not.** When a live stream's function returns an error and the cause of its context is `ErrServerClosed`, an SSE response gets its terminating chunk and the connection closes; a live `Stream` that is not SSE ends with no terminating chunk, so a client cannot take it for a complete body (R5). An SSE function that has sent nothing yet still ends as a 200 with an empty event stream, not a 500. An application sees the cause with the context's own `Cause()` method, not `context.Cause` ([ADR 0016](0016-the-handler-context-is-cancelled-on-disconnect.md)). Cost: a live stream stores its connection's state twice (when it starts and when its function returns) and reads the closing flag once; a plain request and a plain `Stream` store nothing, and the 2 allocations of a typed JSON request and the 4 of a stream are unchanged. What it fails at: a handler that ignores its context holds its connection until Shutdown's context ends; a stream that ends in the instant between Shutdown reading its state and cancelling can cancel the context of the connection's next request, which needs a pipelined request to be waiting on a connection whose answer carried no `Connection: close` (a claim, not tested); and the jitter spreads reconnects only as far as the clients are well behaved (a client that retries at once still retries at once, but now not all in the same millisecond).

**Services around this order** (decided 2026-11-04, [ADR 0079](0079-services-start-after-the-app-binds-and-stop-in-reverse-around-shutdown.md)). A `Service` registered with `app.Service` has a `BeforeShutdown` that runs when Shutdown starts (after readiness fails, before the delay and before the listener closes) and a `Stop` that runs after everything above has finished, both in reverse registration order; `Listen` and `Serve` return `ErrServerClosed` only after the `Stop` calls, so a `main` that waits on `Listen` does not exit in the middle of them. `Close` runs `Stop` with a done context and not `BeforeShutdown`. The framework still installs no signal handler.
