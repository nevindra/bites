# Bodies, streaming, upload, compression: stress test of five recommendations

Machine: AMD Ryzen 7 9700X, Go 1.27.2, probes pinned with `taskset -c 0,1,8,9`, benchmarks run 3 times (spread under 5% unless stated). Probes are in this folder (its own `go.mod`, `replace` to `../../..`): `enum_test.go`, `gzip_test.go`, `multipart_test.go`, `deadline_test.go`, `lowat_test.go`, `usertimeout_test.go`, `openapi_test.go`. Run: `cd docs/research/decisions-bodies && go test -run . -bench . -v`. Commit under test: working tree on top of 79d0e58. Everything marked "measured" has a run in these files; everything else is a claim.

## R3: a stream's write deadline is set per flush, only the total is lifted

Design as implementable: the engine's stream writer buffers; each time its buffer reaches the socket (a `Flush`, or the buffer filling) it calls `SetWriteDeadline(now+WriteTimeout)` before the write; the total duration of the handler function has no deadline; a failed write cancels the context.

Verdict: MODIFY. Keep the per-flush deadline, but it is not enough alone, and three details change.

1. **A flush is a chunk of at most 64 KiB.** A Go write deadline covers the whole `Write` call, so a flush of N bytes demands N/WriteTimeout of throughput: a 10 MiB flush at 30 s needs 340 KiB/s and kills a legitimate 1 Mbit/s client; 64 KiB needs 2.2 KiB/s. The engine splits what the function flushes (claim from how Go deadlines work; the chunk loop is not built).
2. **The deadline alone does not stop a slow reader of small events.** Measured: a client that never reads absorbs 4.1 MiB (about 42,000 events of 100 B) before the first flush blocks, and only then does the deadline start; a stream at 1 event a second holds the connection for hours first. A reader that trickles 1 byte every 50 ms behaves the same (4.1 MiB of 4 KiB flushes accepted, closed 440 ms after the buffer filled; 64 KiB flushes: 2.5 MiB, closed after 400 ms). That is the "reads one byte a minute" slowloris: the connection and up to 4 MiB of kernel send buffer (claim for a remote peer; measured on loopback) stay held.
3. **Set `TCP_NOTSENT_LOWAT` to 16 KiB when a stream starts** (`syscall.SetsockoptInt` through `SyscallConn`, stdlib only, once per stream, not on the plain path). Measured: the accepted backlog of a never-reading client falls from 4,132 KiB to 117 KiB (35 times less); `SO_SNDBUF=64KiB` gives 260 KiB but caps the bandwidth-delay product of downloads. Throughput of a 64 KiB write loop on loopback is unchanged (14.8 to 15.0 GB/s both; WAN throughput not measured, claim that it is unchanged because the option bounds unsent data, not data in flight).
4. **Optional and Linux only: `TCP_USER_TIMEOUT` = WriteTimeout** closes a never-reading and a trickling reader with no per-flush deadline at all. Measured with a 1 s value: write failed after 1.47 to 1.49 s with "connection timed out" for a reader at 0, 1 B/50 ms and 1 B/400 ms. It is the better defence against a slow reader of rare events and costs nothing per flush, but the in-memory entry point (ADR 0027) and the fake clock (ADR 0049) cannot test it, and it is not portable, so it complements the per-flush deadline and does not replace it.

Cost per flush: measured `SetWriteDeadline(time.Now().Add(30s))` 114 ns (the profile's 75 ns plus the clock read); a 100 B loopback write is 1.35 to 1.48 µs without and 1.53 to 1.55 µs with the arm (about +0.15 µs, +10%). Re-arming only when less than half of WriteTimeout remains makes it near zero for a busy stream and one arm per flush for a heartbeat every 15 s, and every flush still has at least WriteTimeout/2 (claim; cheap to build). Do not clear the deadline after each flush (that is a second 100 ns arm and nothing waits on it); clear it once when the stream ends. This respects profile-fib's rule of not adding arms per feature: a stream is the only path that arms per flush and an idle stream arms nothing.

Scenarios: SSE idle for minutes with heartbeats is fine (a heartbeat flush arms, writes into the buffer, returns; nothing waits; idle memory unchanged). A stream that sends rarely and never heartbeats arms nothing while idle. A handler blocked in its own work is not a write problem: the context cancels on disconnect (ADR 0016) and on Shutdown (step 13).

Alternative considered: lift the deadline entirely (ADR 0030 as written). Fails the 4.1 MiB, hours-long hold above and holds a connection for ever; the roadmap already says so. Edits: ADR 0030 ("the write timeout is lifted for that response" becomes "applies to every chunk; the total is lifted"), ADR 0024 table row "write: a streaming response lifts it for itself" becomes "a streaming response applies it to every chunk of at most 64 KiB".

Fails at: a peer that vanishes without RST and without a full buffer (caught by TCP keepalive or USER_TIMEOUT, not by the deadline); a flush rate that makes the 64 KiB chunk size wrong for a real-time client (tunable constant).

## R4: an Upload route gets its own defaults (about 32 MiB, 60 s) instead of a Registration refusal

Design as implementable: `Upload` body kind in `bodyPlan`; when `MaxBody` and `BodyTimeout` are not given for the route or its groups, Registration uses 32 MiB and 60 s and the start-up log prints them marked `default`.

Verdict: MODIFY. Keep the refusal for `MaxBody`, give only the time a default, and add a per-route in-flight option.

Evidence:

- **Is an Upload never buffered whole in the real code? Not yet, and `ReadBody` cannot be the path.** `Exchange.ReadBody` calls `slab.Reserve(n)` with the declared Content-Length before one body byte arrives (`readLength`), and for chunked input `readChunked` accumulates the whole decoded body in `sc.stage` and then copies it into the slab (twice the size). At a raised `MaxBody` of 32 MiB that is a 32 MiB `make` per request on arrival of a header. Measured: 100 untouched `make([]byte, 32 MiB)` is HeapAlloc +3,199 MiB at once; resident pages ranged from 14 MB to 174 MB across runs depending on whether the runtime had to zero reused spans. So the same hole exists for a `[]byte` or `Raw[T]` route with a raised `MaxBody`, and (side finding, claim for the multiplication) `handle()` already sizes the slab at head plus Content-Length up to 1 MiB before the body is read, so 10,000 connections that declare 1 MiB and send nothing account 10 GiB of heap. Step 11 needs its own streaming read path (a part reader over `c.sc.buf` and `c.nc`), and step 8/9 should consider growing the slab as the body arrives rather than trusting the header.
- **What mime/multipart buffers per part (measured).** Streaming a 32 MiB part with `NextPart` and `io.Copy` allocates 18,464 B in 30 mallocs in total (1,817 body bytes per allocated byte): streaming holds. A tiny upload costs 804 ns, 5,156 B, 15 allocs (bufio 4 KiB). 50,000 tiny parts cost 674 B and 8 mallocs per part and `NextPart` has no part-count limit (only `ReadForm` does).
- **But `mime/multipart` has no usable header limit (measured).** `NextPart` allows 10 MiB of headers and 10,000 fields per part. A part header of 8 MiB allocates 9.1 MiB in 16,824 mallocs; one 9 MiB header line allocates 61.7 MB (6.9 times its size). So "limits on parts, part headers and boundary" (step 11) cannot be set around the standard reader: either a small own streaming parser with a header cap (say 8 KiB, 16 fields), a part cap and a boundary of at most 70 bytes, or a size-capping wrapper that is wrong during the data phase. Recommended: own parser (stdlib only; `mime/multipart` also does not import `net/http`, measured 0 matches in `go list -deps`).
- **Hostile client, 32 MiB times MaxConns.** Memory is not the bound when parts stream: per upload about 4 KiB of read buffer plus the handler's own work; the aggregate is limited by bandwidth times body time (a 10 Gbps link carries 75 GB in 60 s), not by 32 MiB times 900,000 connections. Disk is: a handler that spools each part to a temp file lets an unauthenticated client fill the disk at line rate for 60 s. The framework cannot see that, so a bound on concurrent uploads is the only framework-level lever. ADR 0024 puts a concurrency limit under "Not here, waits for a workload that needs one"; Upload is that workload. Recommended: a route and group option `MaxInFlight(n)` (positive, refused at 0 like `MaxBody(0)`), an atomic counter checked after the head and before `100 Continue` is sent, answering 503 with `Retry-After` and closing, so the client never sends the body. No default (a default would 503 legitimate traffic), but the start-up log prints "in flight: unlimited" for an Upload route so the choice is visible. Cost: one atomic add and one atomic sub, a few ns (claim, not benchmarked).
- **60 s total is a rate floor of 4.3 Mbit/s for 32 MiB.** A phone at 1 Mbit/s gets a 408 after 60 s with the file 80% sent. An upload default time should be derived from the size: `max(30 s, MaxBody / 128 KiB/s)` (256 s for 32 MiB), logged as default (claim; the floor of 128 KiB/s is a judgement).
- **Why not default the size.** 32 MiB is wrong in both directions for real routes (an avatar wants 2 MiB, a video wants 2 GiB), and the failure of a default is a 413 in production, found by a user, where a refusal is found at start-up. The refusal text names the route and the missing option, so the DX cost is one option on the route: `MaxBody(32<<20)`. That also matches ADR 0007 and ADR 0024's "a limit is never switched off by accident" and keeps the one number about the application's storage a decision of the application's author.

Compared: default 32 MiB / 60 s (DX: zero lines, risk: silent acceptance of 32 MiB from anyone, 408 for slow clients); refusal of both (DX: two lines, no surprises); chosen: refusal of `MaxBody`, derived default time, optional `MaxInFlight`. Edits: ADR 0044 last sentence, ADR 0024 (upload time, `MaxInFlight`), roadmap open item.

Fails at: a chunked upload has no Content-Length so the 413 comes only after `MaxBody` bytes were read (acceptable: the bytes streamed, not buffered); the rate-floor default is unmeasured.

## Enum on output: declared, not enforced at encode

Verdict: KEEP (ADR 0042 says so), with the cost on record. Measured on top of the existing 5.3 ns `AppendQuote` of a string: enforcing by linear scan costs +2.7 ns for 3 values, +5.6 ns for 8, +6.9 ns for 32 (last value), and a map costs +3.9 ns at 32; no allocation in any case. A response with 20 enum fields pays 50 to 140 ns, about 1 to 3% of the 3.5 to 4 µs a typical request spends in CPU per profile-fib (claim for the share). So cost is not the reason to refuse enforcement; the reasons are semantic: a bad value would be a 500 for the whole response (the response is buffered in `r.body`, so this is possible) because a database row held a value the Go type's `Values()` does not list (an old row after a deploy, a migration in progress), which turns a data drift into an outage for a field the client may not even use; decode must refuse (the client sent it), encode need not. Alternative considered: enforce in tests only, via a helper in `nilotest` that checks a response value against `Values()`; recommended as the middle path (claim, not built). ADR 0042 should say: an enum on a response is described in the OpenAPI document and not checked when written. Fails at: a client generated from the document that rejects an unknown value (the usual open-enum hazard).

## OpenAPI: `[]byte` and `Raw[T]` bodies through the generator's Body hook

Verdict: MODIFY for `[]byte`, REJECT for `Raw[T]`.

- **`Raw[T]` is not binary.** In the code (`compile.go`, `bodyRaw`) and in ADR 0044, `Raw[T]` requires `application/json`, decodes into T and keeps the bytes beside it. Its schema is T's schema as JSON, which is what the existing hook does (it sees through `Raw[T]`). Describing it as `application/octet-stream` would be wrong and would make generated clients send it unvalidated. The roadmap sentence "a `[]byte` or `Raw[T]` body is raw bytes" should say `[]byte` only.
- **`[]byte` as the body root is raw bytes with any content type** (ADR 0044: accepts any). Measured today: `Schema([]byte, Request)` returns `{"contentEncoding":"base64","type":"string"}` (also for `[16]byte`), correct for a field in a JSON object, wrong for the body root. The current `Body func(reflect.Type) (reflect.Type, bool)` cannot express this because it maps a type to a type; there is no type whose schema is binary. Change the hook to return a body description (a schema plus a media type, or "raw"): for a `[]byte` request body emit `content: {"*/*": {}}` (OpenAPI 3.1 says a binary body needs no schema, and `format: binary` is a 3.0 form that 3.1 replaced by `contentMediaType`); if a schema is wanted, `{"type":"string","contentMediaType":"application/octet-stream"}`.
- **`[]byte` fields inside JSON stay base64**, as the codec writes them: unchanged, tested above for `Avatar.Data`.
- **A `[]byte` response is not a feature of the current return types**: a handler that returns a `[]byte` today gets a JSON base64 string (jsoncodec's delegate path), so there is no octet-stream response to describe until a raw-bytes response type is decided (a `Bytes`/`File`-like wrapper, ADR 0013 and step 11's `File`). Fails at: the Body-hook contract change touches `internal/openapi` and its 12 goldens.

## gzip (step 15): off by default, bodies over about 1 KiB, content types that compress, pooled writers inside the engine

Verdict: KEEP with two fixed numbers: BestSpeed, threshold 1 KiB.

Measured (JSON list of records, synthetic and more repetitive than real data, so ratios are optimistic; time is compress of an in-memory body into a reused buffer, steady state, 0 allocs):

| body | default level (-1) | BestSpeed (1) |
|---|---|---|
| 607 B | 4.0 µs, 196 B out | 3.2 µs, 200 B |
| 1,087 B | 4.9 µs, 250 B | 3.5 µs, 257 B |
| 4,111 B | 9.9 µs, 604 B | 5.5 µs, 613 B |
| 16,468 B | 29.7 µs, 1,819 B | 13.4 µs, 1,983 B (+9%) |
| 65,558 B | 107 µs, 5,761 B | 38.3 µs, 6,839 B (+19%) |

- **Writer cost.** `gzip.NewWriterLevel` per response: 31 to 33 µs and 748 KB in 13 allocs at default, 19 to 21 µs and 486 KB at BestSpeed. A `sync.Pool` `Get`, `Reset`, `Close`, `Put`: 33.5 ns, 0 bytes, 0 allocs at both levels. So the pool is mandatory; without it a 1 KiB response costs 6 times its own compression in construction and 486 KB of garbage.
- **Level.** BestSpeed is 1.4 times faster at 1 KiB, 1.8 at 4 KiB, 2.2 at 16 KiB, 2.8 at 64 KiB for 0 to 19% more output; recommend `BestSpeed`, fixed, no level knob in the first version.
- **Threshold.** At 1 KiB the cost is 3.5 µs for 830 B saved. A response of head plus 1 KiB already fits one TCP segment (MSS about 1,448), so compression below roughly 1.2 KiB saves bytes but not packets or round trips; the saving in round trips begins above one segment and matters most when a body crosses the 10-segment initial window (about 14 KB), which is the 16 KiB row (claim, wire effect not measured). Keep 1 KiB as the default of an option `MinSize`; do not go below it.
- **Cost against the budget.** 13.4 µs at 16 KiB is 3 to 4 times the whole CPU of a typical request in profile-fib; off by default and only above the threshold means ADR 0003's 10% throughput margin is not spent by default, but the ADR must say the cost is paid per compressed response, not hidden in the 10%. Allocations: 0 added at steady state (measured), so the 2-allocation row holds; one more pooled scratch buffer for the compressed output lives in the engine's scratch, like `sc.stage`, and must be dropped past `maxPooled` on release.
- **Memory (ADR 0005).** A pooled writer holds 0.5 to 0.75 MB, but compression is CPU-bound and never blocks (it works on `r.body` after `Serve` returned), so live writers are about GOMAXPROCS, not per connection: 16 writers is about 8 MB at BestSpeed (claim from that argument; idle memory per connection is untouched). `sync.Pool` empties after two GCs and a refill costs 21 µs and 486 KB (measured), acceptable.
- **ADR 0008 and 0031.** The pool sits in `write()` of the engine on the engine's own buffer; no writer, buffer or compressed slice reaches user code, and ADR 0009 holds (the decision is made from the return value's response plus `Accept-Encoding`). Rules to write in ADR 0031: compress only when `Accept-Encoding` lists gzip; skip if `Content-Encoding` is already set, status is 204/304 or the method is HEAD, the type is already compressed (image, video, zip), or the handler set `Cache-Control: no-transform`; always add `Vary: Accept-Encoding` (also on responses not compressed because of size); weaken strong ETags; never compress a `Stream` or SSE (a flush per event is not what gzip is for; not measured); and document BREACH (a response that reflects a secret next to attacker input) in the guide. `compress/gzip` imports no `net/http` (measured), so ADR 0006 and ADR 0019 hold.

Fails at: streaming responses and Range (milestone 3) are not compressed; a 16 KiB response costs 13 µs of CPU per request on a machine that is CPU-bound, so an app behind a compressing load balancer should leave it off, which is the default.
